Every control has limits. It gets interesting at exactly the point where somebody wants to go past them.

The cheapest answer is nothing. The handle stops, the finger keeps moving, the connection breaks. For a fraction of a second the user is holding something that no longer responds.

The expensive answer is to yield.

## Why logarithmic

Linear resistance feels like a broken control: pull twice as far and you get twice the travel, just beyond the maximum. That is not a limit, that is a second scale.

Logarithmic resistance gives the first few pixels away almost for free and then less and less:

```ts
function rubberband(distance: number) {
  return Math.sign(distance) * Math.min(26, Math.log1p(Math.abs(distance)) * 7);
}
```

The cap at 26 px matters. Without it the travel keeps growing — slowly, but visibly. With it you get the feeling of a material that stretches and then, at some point, does not.

## The second half: coming back

The stretch is half the work. The other half is the release. A spring with too little damping oscillates and feels rubbery. One with too much crawls back and feels sluggish.

<Callout>
Rule of thumb: critically damped plus ten percent. One overshoot, barely visible — no more.
</Callout>

Nobody will tell you they noticed. They will say the product feels good, and they will not be able to name a reason.
