There are two kinds of animation in an interface. One wants to be seen. The other wants to explain. Almost everything a product needs belongs to the second kind — and gets built like the first anyway.

A movement explains when it answers a question the user would otherwise have to answer themselves: *Where did that come from? Where did it go? Do those things still belong together?*

## The test

Take any animation in your product and halve its duration. If nothing is lost, it was too long. Repeat until something breaks.

<Callout>
Most transitions in shipping interfaces sit between 400 and 600 ms. Nearly all of them work better at 180.
</Callout>

The difference from three separate backgrounds fading in and out is tiny in code and large in perception. A travelling object creates continuity. Two cross-fading objects create a cut.

## What I have settled on

- **Springs over curves** for anything following a gesture. A `cubic-bezier` cannot know how fast the finger was.
- **Curves over springs** for anything that happens on its own. Here determinism is a feature, not a shortcoming.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` **not as an off switch** but as a swap: position instead of movement, opacity instead of displacement.

Motion is not ornament applied at the end. It is the grammar an interface uses to narrate its own changes of state.
